## Context

For decades, an NIH-funded study had followed families across rural Appalachia. When I joined, it was losing participants faster than it could replace them, and its funding depended on the data they provided.

Surveys were done on paper bubble sheets. The sheets traveled between sites for a week or more before someone typed them into the database by hand, and errors crept in along the way. The questions were dense and academic, more than 120 of them, written for readers the study rarely met. Many participants read with difficulty, knew little medical vocabulary, and had good reason to be wary of institutions.

<SurveyTimeline />

**My role:** UX consultant at p2design. I led design from field research through in-clinic testing over a seven-week engagement, with updates through the following year. I worked with research teams at Pitt and WVU, clinicians, developers, and community partners.

## Objective

Reliable data came first. On the paper sheets, one pattern kept appearing: whole columns of identical answers, filled in by people who wanted to be done. Those surveys were complete, and the study couldn't use them.

Participants needed questions they could follow by ear and answer without reading or writing. They also needed their exam visit to run on time. Clinicians needed accurate records without losing hours to paperwork. All of it had to work in rural clinics with:

- **Limited literacy.** Nothing could rely on reading or writing.
- **Unreliable internet.** The tablets had to work offline and sync later.
- **Thin trust.** A participant who had a bad visit might not come back.

## The original work

### 1. Turning 120 questions into speech

**Decision:** Rewrite every question in plain spoken language. Participants listen and tap instead of reading and writing.

I worked through the survey item by item with the research team. I handled the wording and the interaction, and the researchers made sure each rewrite still measured what the original was validated to measure.

| Original | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| "Is there any known consanguinity?" | "Are the child's parents related by blood?" |
| "Most people do not realize the extent to which their illnesses are controlled by accidental happenings." | "Getting sick is mostly a matter of chance, not something you control." |
| "I know someone I would feel perfectly comfortable talking to about problems budgeting my time between school and my social life." | "When balancing school and friends gets hard, I have someone I'm comfortable talking to." |

Most of the effort went into protecting each question's meaning while the words got simpler. The rewrites were recorded as audio prompts and loaded onto tablets, which ran offline and uploaded to a shared database at the end of each clinic day.

### 2. Watching the clinic day

**Decision:** With the tablets in use, study the whole clinic day.

I spent time in the clinic watching visits unfold from arrival to exam. Two problems had nothing to do with the survey:

- **Onboarding.** Clinicians spent about 20 minutes with each household, collecting names and drawing three-generation family trees by hand.
- **The exam queue.** Emergencies took priority, as they should, and families with routine exams waited for hours.

I then mapped the full service, from the participant's path to staff workflows and the handoffs between them, to see how the two problems were connected.

*[Visual: the "before" service blueprint, with both problems marked.]*

### 3. Family trees in a minute

**Decision:** Participants choose their household members from a list, and the system draws the family tree.

Onboarding went from 20 minutes per household to 1. Clinicians spent that time checking families in.

*[Visual: animation (Lottie or Figma Smart Animate) of household selection building the family tree.]*

### 4. Clearing the exam backlog

**Decision:** Bring a staffing problem to the study's directors, even though it sat outside my scope.

When someone arrived with an emergency, such as an infection, clinicians treated them first. The families behind them waited, sometimes for hours, and some went home without an exam.

A screen couldn't solve this, and staffing belonged to the directors. I brought it to them in the study's terms: each family that left was a participant, and a set of data, the study might not get back. We agreed to add an overflow dental team for emergencies. Urgent cases still got care, and scheduled exams started on time.

## Outcomes

The study recovered enough participation and data quality to keep its NIH funding.

## What I learned

**Map the whole service before designing screens.**

The blueprint came after I'd already seen the problems in the clinic. It explained them, but drawn earlier, it would have found them sooner. Now I map the whole service first, and that habit shaped the rebuild.

## The rebuild: the survey and the clinic as one system

**Decision:** Start from the service map and design the survey and the clinic together.

The first version solved them separately, with tablets for the survey and a new team for emergencies. In the rebuild, the participant's tablet and the clinic's dashboard share the same data, so staff can see the day as it happens.

*[Visual: the "after" service blueprint, with the original problems resolved.]*

**My role:** I designed and coded the rebuild in React and Next.js. The ElevenLabs API generates each question's audio ahead of time so the tablets work offline. The Anthropic API drafts plain-language versions of questions for researchers to review.

**Design principle:** All AI work happens before clinic day. Nothing at the clinic needs an internet connection.

### When a question doesn't land

**Decision:** Simpler versions of each question are written and approved ahead of time. None are generated during a session.

If a participant replays a question twice, the tablet offers a simpler version. Each validated question measures one specific thing, and loose rewording can change what it measures without anyone noticing. A model drafts the alternatives, and researchers approve each one before it goes on a tablet.

After a few questions, the tablet calls the participant to the dental chair. When they come back, the survey resumes where they stopped. People answer questions during time they would otherwise spend waiting.

### When someone gets tired

**Decision:** Detect fatigue with simple rules on the tablet instead of a model.

The tablet compares how long each answer takes against that person's own pace, and counts replays. It also watches for the old paper problem: answers tapped before the question finishes, or the same answer chosen again and again. When those signs appear, the tablet offers a break or a simpler version, and flags the dashboard so staff can check in. Rules run offline, and researchers can see exactly why a flag was raised.

### Seeing the clinic day at a glance

**Decision:** Put the clinic's whole day on one screen for the clinical lead.

- Today's participants, with details for each person
- Exams completed, pending, and in the queue
- Who is taking surveys, and how far along they are
- Where staff are: dental lab, survey area, or front desk
- Fatigue flags and help requests

The lead can spot a growing queue early and move staff before families start leaving.
