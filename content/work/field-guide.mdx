> **PROJECT SUMMARY**
> A decades-long NIH-funded health study in Appalachia was losing participants faster than it could replace them, and with them, its data and its funding. I led a voice-first redesign of the entire data-collection experience: plain-language spoken surveys for low-literacy participants, digitized onboarding, and a fix to the broken care promise that had eroded trust. Participation and data quality recovered enough to keep the study viable.

## At a glance

- **Who:** Low-literacy rural participants in an NIH-funded Appalachian health study, plus the Pitt and WVU research teams and clinicians running it
- **What:** A voice-first, offline-capable field data-collection system replacing 120+ question paper surveys, plus a redesigned clinic service flow
- **Result:** Restored participation, data quality, and trust enough to protect the study and its NIH funding

## The outcome

### The study wasn't failing on science. It was failing on data and trust.

The research itself was sound. What was collapsing was the pipeline feeding it. Paper surveys came back riddled with errors. Participants couldn't parse 120+ dense academic questions. Families who waited all day for promised dental care left without it.

Every broken promise cost trust, and every lost participant cost data. A decades-long, NIH-funded study was one bad season away from losing the participation, and the funding, it ran on.

The redesign protected the one thing the institution could not afford to lose: a study that still produced usable data. Participation recovered, data quality recovered, and the model is now being looked at for replication at other underserved sites.

## The users, and what they needed

### Built for people the usual research tools were never designed to reach

Participants carried low digital and health literacy, dense academic language they couldn't decode, and years of institutional mistrust. They needed three things:

- **To understand what was being asked.** Plain, spoken language instead of academic prose.
- **To answer without reading or writing.** Voice-first, not form-first.
- **To actually receive the care they were promised** for showing up.

The researchers and clinicians had their own need: accurate, transferable data without losing hours to manual onboarding.

## My role

### Design lead, from field research to in-clinic testing

I researched field conditions and participant experience, redesigned the survey and onboarding, and tested and adapted the system on site, in real clinics, in real time.

I worked with research teams, clinicians, developers, and community partners. The community partners weren't stakeholders to manage. They were the reason any of it worked, because in this setting trust ran through them, not through the tool.

## The constraints

### The field, not the lab

- **Extreme low literacy.** Nothing could depend on a participant reading or writing.
- **No reliable connectivity.** The system had to run offline and sync later.
- **Fragile trust.** One more broken promise and participation would not come back.

## The work

### From a 120-question academic survey to a spoken conversation

The old instrument asked participants things like whether there was "known consanguinity." Working alongside the research team, I ran the survey question by question, and together we reworked each one into a plain-language sound bite a participant could take in the first time they heard it. My job was the translation and the interaction; theirs was guarding what each item was scientifically there to measure. Then it all moved onto tablet-based voice prompts people could listen to and answer out loud, with no reading required.

A few of the rewrites, side by side:

| Original | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| "Is there any known consanguinity?" | "Are the child's parents related by blood?" |
| "Most people do not realize the extent to which their illnesses are controlled by accidental happenings." | "Getting sick is mostly a matter of chance, not something you control." |
| "I know someone I would feel perfectly comfortable talking to about problems budgeting my time between school and my social life." | "When balancing school and friends gets hard, I have someone I'm comfortable talking to." |

The discipline wasn't making the words shorter. It was preserving exactly what each validated question measured, a health belief, a sense of control, the presence of support, while making it answerable by someone who couldn't parse the original. That is where the design lived. Not in the styling, in the translation.

### Digitizing onboarding to unblock the clinicians

On site, I found clinicians spending hours hand-collecting family names and drawing three-level family trees by hand for every household. I redesigned that flow so the system generated the pedigree automatically once data was entered. That cut wait times and freed clinical staff to do clinical work.

*[Visual: The auto-generated family tree — input on one side, generated pedigree on the other, the systems-thinking proof.]*

### Fixing the care promise, because trust is a systems problem, not a UI problem

Dental needs were severe, and families kept leaving without care after waiting all day. No interface change fixes that, and it wasn't a decision I owned. So I built the argument and took it to the directors: stand up a dedicated overflow dental team to absorb emergencies in parallel, so urgent cases were always covered without disrupting the scheduled appointment flow. I made the case in their terms, that every family turned away was participation and data walking out the door, and they backed it. After that, people got care every time they showed up. Trust recovered, and participation followed it.

## Key decisions and trade-offs

| Decision | Why | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice-first over form-first | Reading and writing were barriers, not options, for these participants | Survey became usable for the population it was meant to reach |
| Auto-generated pedigrees | Manual family-tree drawing was consuming clinician hours | Onboarding dropped from hours to minutes |
| Bring in overflow dental teams | You can't design trust into a screen; it's earned through kept promises | Attendance and trust recovered |

## The lesson

### Design didn't live in the screen. It lived in the system.

The interface mattered, but the participation problem got solved by aligning three things at once: the technology, the people, and the promises. Accessibility here wasn't a feature, it was the entire job. And trust couldn't be designed into a UI. It could only be earned through service that showed up consistently.

### Map the whole service first, before you touch a screen

I didn't discover the onboarding bottleneck until I was standing in a clinic watching clinicians hand-draw family trees. That was too late. If I had blueprinted the entire service up front, the participant journey, the staff workflows, and every handoff between them, I would have seen the manual pedigree work and the care-delivery gap on day one instead of finding them mid-project. The redesign still landed, but mapping the system early would have gotten me there faster and with far less rework. It's the first thing I do now.

## How I'd build this today

The core moves would be the same. The tools would not.

The part the research team and I did by hand, rewriting 120+ validated questions into plain-language sound bites without breaking what each one measured, is now something a language model can draft in seconds. But that just moves the design problem up a level. An LLM left alone will happily simplify an item into something that no longer measures what it's supposed to, so the real work becomes the guardrail: the model proposes plain-language versions, the researchers confirm construct validity, and nothing ships without that check. It's the same suggest-then-confirm discipline I used for the AI x-ray reads, pointed at survey design.

From there the survey stops being a fixed script and becomes adaptive. Fatigue detection would catch the moment a participant's attention drops, the exact point where my journey map showed people getting tired and hungry, and slow down or pause instead of pushing all 120 questions in one sitting. Regional voice personas would let the system speak in a voice that sounds local, because in a community that runs on trust, who is asking matters as much as what is asked. And it would still work offline first and sync when it can, because the connectivity problem hasn't gone anywhere.

What wouldn't change is the starting point. The goal was never to automate the interview. It was to make research feel human to people the system usually fails. Today's tools just let me hold that goal at a scale I couldn't reach with a tablet and a speech SDK.
