# The machine can be complicated. The operator experience can't.

I design the human side of autonomy. My work spans enterprise software, robotics, and physical AI: translating what a machine is doing into something a person can read, trust, and act on with confidence.

## Designing for the way people actually think.

Most intelligent systems are designed around how engineers assume operators think, not how people actually reason when they're under pressure. That's where my second craft comes in.

Coaching and product design look like different crafts. They aren't. Both ask the same question: what is this person actually trying to do, and what's getting in the way? A coaching conversation is discovery with language as the only tool.

Two decades of design and hundreds of conversations taught me to hear the problem beneath the problem. It's a refined skill that taught me how to suspend all assumptions and biases.

## The systems I design

Autonomous systems create a new category of design problem: a person has to read what a machine is doing, trust what it's reporting, and act, often under pressure, with incomplete information. I'm interested in the design principles that make that possible. How confidence gets communicated. Where authority should sit. What honest machine state actually looks like.

## What's next

Physical AI is where software meets the real world. Drones, robots, and autonomous systems don't just generate information. They create situations where people have to act on it, with real consequences attached.

That's the problem I'm built for, and where I want to spend the next chapter of my career: setting design direction for human-machine interfaces in physical AI, building the teams that deliver them, and making the case for that work where it gets funded.

If you're building autonomy that has to work in someone's hands, not just perform on a benchmark, I'd like to talk.
