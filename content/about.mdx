# Most AI builders start with the system. I started with the human.

<AboutPhoto />

I'm a senior product designer working at the intersection of AI systems and human behavior. Organizations bring me ambiguous, high-stakes problems, like a research study drowning in paper or a conversation no tool can hold, and I turn them into shipped systems people trust.

One question has followed me everywhere: why does software keep failing the humans it's supposed to serve?

It led me to Human-Computer Interaction at Carnegie Mellon, and learning to code, because you can't understand how a system fails without knowing how it's built. Then it became personal. An accident left my partner with a traumatic brain injury. Watching his mind rebuild while mine held everything together changed what I was looking for. I stopped building software and started studying the system it's supposed to serve: the human mind.

Coaching was the laboratory: hundreds of hours watching in real time what people can actually take in, where trust breaks, and how language does the work. Language is how humans make meaning. It turned out to be the missing half of every technical system I'd built.

Common Project is where the two threads converged. Taking notes during sessions broke presence with the client, so I built something that didn't require it: a tool that captures each utterance, analyzes it, plots it, and shows where the conversation is going in real time. No notebook, full presence. That work made something clear: to understand something, I need to build a model.

When large language models emerged, the recognition was immediate. Language, studied at the depth of practice, had become the primary material of AI systems. Not a trend. The tool this work had always been waiting for.

That thinking has shipped as NIH-funded research infrastructure serving a 700-household cohort study, and as Paloma AI, a coaching companion built to close the gap between who someone is and who they want to be. 83% of beta users return weekly.
