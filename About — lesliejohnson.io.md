# About — lesliejohnson.io

## Most AI products are built to be believed. I build them to be checked.

Confidence is cheap to render. Any interface can fake it: a tidy percentage, a clean recommendation, a progress bar that fills right on schedule. None of that is knowledge. Which makes the industry's goal the wrong one. The job isn't getting people to trust autonomous systems. It's making their trust accurate, so it holds when the machine is right and gives way when it isn't.

A drone reports a damaged roof. A dashboard recommends a change. Somewhere a person has a few seconds to decide whether to believe it, and no way to tell whether the machine is sure or just fluent. That person is who I design for. Then I build it.

## What accurate trust costs

### 1. A system that never says "I don't know" has already taught you to stop checking.

Uniform confidence is a design failure, not a polish problem. For a financial advisor, I built an agent that answers only from his own documents and hands the question back to him the moment it's unsure. Most teams would call that handback a gap to close. It's the opposite. It's the only reason the other answers are worth anything.

### 2. Trust is earned where the work happens, which is seldom where the design happens.

I designed Field Guide for an NIH-funded health study in rural Appalachia. There was no brief. The team suspected that digitizing a 120-question paper survey might help and had no idea how to move. So I went and watched, then designed for the conditions nobody had written down: no reliable wifi, private health data, three institutions that all needed the results. Tablets ran offline and traveled with the team. Data was copied at checkout, uploaded nightly, and every tablet wiped.

83% of participants completed the survey in full. The study kept its funding. And because I staggered arrivals and added an overflow dentist station, every participant was seen by a dentist. Before that, 23% were sent home to reschedule.

### 3. Nobody designs the handoff. That's exactly where it breaks.

Failures rarely live on the screen. They live in the second responsibility moves between a person and a machine, when nobody specified what the person is supposed to know at that moment. Teams ship the capability and leave the decision undesigned.

As the sole product designer at a Global Payments company, I redesigned how students paid their loans inside a compliance-heavy regulated product. Abandoned payments fell 27%. Support calls fell 43%. The flow became the primary self-service channel. I've since prototyped operator consoles for drone fleets and data centers, and I build design systems that tell both people and AI agents what to show, and when.
